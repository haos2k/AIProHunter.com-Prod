What Is a Minimalist Entrepreneur? Start from Community, Not from Product

Apr 14, 2026·5 min read

When most people start thinking about entrepreneurship, the first question spinning in their head is: "What product should I build?" — then an idea pops up, they start building, and a few months later they discover nobody actually wants to buy it.

Sahil Lavingia says in The Minimalist Entrepreneur: you were wrong from the very first step.

What Is a Minimalist Entrepreneur

A Minimalist Entrepreneur isn't a lesson in frugality — "do business with as little money as possible" — though it does happen to be cheaper. It's more like a reordering of entrepreneurial philosophy: put "helping real people solve real problems" first, and put "product" second.

The difference in that order looks like just a few words shifting position, but the decisions and actions that follow are almost completely different.

The traditional entrepreneurship path looks like this: have an idea → build a product → find a market → burn money on promotion → hope someone buys.
The Minimalist Entrepreneur's path is: find a community → observe real pain points → validate that people will pay → only then start building the minimum necessary thing.

One starts from your own imagination. The other starts from other people's reality.

Why Start from Community

Lavingia said something worth printing out and putting on your wall: "If you try to make something for everyone, you will likely end up making something that no one really wants or needs."

A product not aimed at any specific group will ultimately become a product nobody owes anything to.

"Community" here doesn't mean you spent three weeks joining a Discord server and can now say "I have a community" — it means the group of people you're already inside, and genuinely care about. It might be colleagues you know from work, creators you've followed for years in a similar space, or a field you've continuously participated in and discussed.

Lavingia suggests asking yourself a few questions to find it:

  • When I talk, who is listening?
  • Where do I spend my time, online and offline?
  • In what context do I feel most like myself?
  • What group of people do I spend time with — even when it's sometimes uncomfortable — because we share something more important?

Where those answers point is your starting point.

Creator First, Entrepreneur Second

This is the most counterintuitive and yet most powerful concept in the Minimalist Entrepreneur methodology: become a creator first, then an entrepreneur.

Not that you need to become a YouTuber or start a podcast — but that before you think about charging money, you should first be the person who is genuinely helping people.

Lavingia says: "Becoming a person who helps people precedes building a business that helps people. It's not a coincidence."

The practical action this maps to is: contribute first. Answer questions in the community, share what you know, help others solve problems you've already solved — not because you're trying to sell something, but because you genuinely care about those people. The trust built this way is something no advertising budget can buy later.

What "Genuinely Observing Pain Points" Looks Like

After spending time in a community, you start seeing real problems — not the ones you "think they might have," but the ones they're complaining about every day, working around, or giving up on.

The key is: don't rush to solve — first get the problem clearly understood.

Lavingia doesn't recommend "going to ask them what they want" — because people find it hard to articulate what they actually need. Instead, observe what they're "patching," what they're "working around," and "why the existing solutions aren't good enough." Sometimes a problem appearing repeatedly across different people is the market talking to you.

What you're looking for isn't "can I solve this problem?" but rather "is this problem painful enough that people would pay to have it solved?"

Common Mistakes

"I don't have a community, so I can't start yet."
You're already in a community — you just haven't recognized it. Where you ask questions, who you talk to, whose articles you follow — these are all clues. "Creating a community" isn't the starting point; "finding the one you're already in" is.

"Once I find a community, I should quickly build a product."
Wrong. The first step after finding a community is: stay in it seriously, observe, contribute — with no commercial intent whatsoever. Rushing to build a product means jumping back to the traditional path.

"My idea is great, and the community is just there to validate it."
If that's the mindset, you're not doing community first — you're using community as packaging for idea first. True community first means: you're uncertain about the solution, you're only certain that you care about these people, and you let their problems guide you.

Next Step

Once you've figured out where to start, the next question is: okay, I've found a real pain point — how much do I need to build before it's "enough" to launch?

That's exactly what Build as Little as Possible answers — the right mindset for minimum viable products, and why running a manual process saves more time than writing code first.
