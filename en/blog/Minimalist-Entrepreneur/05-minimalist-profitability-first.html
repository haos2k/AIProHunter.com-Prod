Profitability First — The Minimalist Entrepreneur's Approach to Building Without VC Money

Apr 14, 2026·6 min read

You know the most common entrepreneurship mantra?
"Get users first — revenue can wait."

Sahil Lavingia has seen what the end of that road looks like — because he walked it himself. Gumroad once raised $20 million, was valued at over $100 million, and had more than thirty employees. Then he had to lay people off, downsize, and nearly shut the company down entirely.

Then he chose a different path — Profitability First. And that path kept Gumroad alive.

Profitability First Isn't Caution — It's Freedom

When many people hear "profitability first," their gut reaction is: that sounds conservative. Is it an unambitious choice?

Lavingia's answer is: it's a form of freedom to choose.

Once you take external funding, you have obligations to investors — their timeline, their return expectations, their definition of "success." This isn't to say VCs are bad people; it's that when you take someone else's money, you have to run at someone else's pace.

Minimalist Entrepreneurs don't want the fastest growth — they want the ability to keep operating without ever depending on anyone else.

Lavingia says: "You don't need external funding — only time until your business generates returns."

Profitability is how you buy back your own time.

What "Profitability First" Thinking Looks Like

This isn't just the obvious statement "I want to make money" — it's a concrete decision-making framework.

Profitability-first thinking means every decision starts by asking: "Does this help me reach or maintain profitability?"

Should I build this feature? See whether it makes existing customers more willing to pay, or makes it easier for new customers to buy.
Should I hire someone? See whether current revenue can support that cost — not "if growth hits target, then yes."
Should I expand into new markets? See whether you've already found a real, profitable model in your current market — not burning cash to grab territory first.

This framework keeps every step grounded in reality — not betting on "once we hit X users it will start making money," but having real revenue supporting every stage.

Pricing: Start Charging Earlier Than You Think

Profitability first has one deeply counterintuitive starting point: start charging before you feel "ready."

Lavingia says many entrepreneurs' problem isn't that they have nothing to sell — it's that they habitually delay charging. Waiting for features to be more complete, for more users, for the product to "feel worth the price."

But here's the truth: you will never feel like "now is exactly the right time to start charging" — because that feeling doesn't appear on its own. You have to actively make the decision.

And when you start charging, you get more than just income — you get real market feedback. Someone paying means the problem you're solving is real and your solution is working. Nobody paying also tells you something important — and you need to know that sooner rather than later.

Your North Star Shouldn't Be User Count

When you make user count your north star, your decisions push toward "get more people using it, regardless of whether they'll pay" — free plans, acquisition features, using growth to mask the absence of profitability.

When you make profitability your north star, your decisions push toward "who cares about this problem enough to pay for it? How can I make their lives better?" — every feature decision is closer to real needs, every customer relationship is healthier.

Lavingia said something very direct: "A business is a way to solve problems for people you care about — and get paid for it."

In that definition, "get paid for it" isn't a footnote — it's the core. If you're solving problems but making no money, that's charity. Not a business. Both have value, but don't confuse them.

Growth Is Not the Goal — It's the Byproduct

This series has been talking about community first since the very first article — Minimalist Entrepreneurs start from community, validate problems, manually serve customers, and gradually build a product. When does growth happen along this entire path?

After each customer is genuinely well-served.

When you've served your first customer well, they refer the second. When you find a model that truly generates profit, you optimize it and replicate it. This kind of growth isn't fast, but every step is solid — you're not betting on the future, you're accumulating in the present.

Lavingia says: "Most people don't start. Most people who start don't continue. Most people who continue give up. Many winners are just the last ones standing. Don't give up."

Minimalist Entrepreneurs don't win by running the fastest — they win by having the ability to keep running. Profitability is the engine that keeps you running.

Common Mistakes

"Without VC you can't build anything big."
That depends on what "big" means to you. Many businesses generating seven figures annually have never taken outside funding — they simply found a real market, served a real group of people well, and built the business steadily year after year. Whether that counts as "big" depends on how you define it.

"Go free first, charge once you're big enough."
The problem with this path: the users you accumulate during the free period came because it was free — not because your product delivered enough value. When you start charging, their first reaction is to leave, not pay. Users who pay from day one give you a far more honest market signal.

"Profitability first will make my growth too slow and let competitors pass me."
Your real competitors aren't the VC-funded companies burning cash — they're playing a game you don't need to join. Your actual competitors are those growing on real revenue just like you, and your best advantage is that you know your customers better.

The End of the Whole Path

If you string the logic of all five articles together, it looks like this:

Find the community you care about → observe their real pain points → validate a solution with minimal resources → find your first customers through educational selling → attract the right people with your authentic self → make every step profitable, let profitability support every step

This isn't a path to a billion-dollar valuation. But it is a path you can truly control, that is truly sustainable, and that will still be running ten years from now.

The last thing Lavingia says, which I think is the essence of this entire methodology: "You don't learn, then start. You start, then learn."

Start first, learn as you go — as long as you're making money, you have the time to keep learning.
