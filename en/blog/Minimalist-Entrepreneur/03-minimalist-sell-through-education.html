Sell Through Education — Let Customers Come to You

Apr 14, 2026·5 min read

When most people hear "sales," their first reaction is: you have to convince someone.

Then they start thinking about how to sound more impressive, how to answer "why is this worth the price," how to handle rejection — and the whole thing turns into a tug-of-war where you talk and the other person defends.

Sahil Lavingia describes sales in The Minimalist Entrepreneur as something completely different: not convincing someone to buy, but figuring out together whether this solution is actually worth buying.

Sales Is a Learning Process

Lavingia's core claim is this: Selling is a discovery process.

He says Minimalist Entrepreneurs treat sales as an opportunity — a chance to talk one-on-one with a potential customer about their problem, while also helping them understand your solution. Both things happen simultaneously, not sell-first then explain.

This shift in mindset changes the entire direction of the conversation.

Traditional sales mindset: I have something, and I need to make you think it's great.
Educational sales mindset: I have a problem I want to understand better, and I have a solution I want you to understand — let's look at these two things together and see if they fit.

One is a battle of offense and defense. The other is collaboration. Customers feel the difference clearly.

How to Turn a Conversation into Education

In practice, Lavingia's educational selling involves a few core moves:

One-on-one, not mass outreach.
At least in the early stages, one-on-one is the most efficient approach. Not because you can't serve more people simultaneously, but because one-on-one lets you hear the real friction and real questions — these are the most valuable raw material for your product copy and content marketing later.

Ask questions first, then introduce your solution.
In the first half of the conversation, your job is to understand the other person's situation: How are they currently dealing with this problem? What have they tried? Where are they stuck? — This isn't mechanically going through a checklist; it's genuine curiosity.

Then, only after you understand their situation, do you bring your solution into the conversation. Every word you say at that point is directed at their specific problem, not spoken into empty air.

Teach, don't push.
How you present the solution matters too. Not "my product can do X, Y, Z for you," but "this problem usually has a few dimensions — the one you mentioned, the approach I've seen work before is… does that direction seem like it might work for you?"

One is a sales pitch; the other is teaching. The other person's defenses are completely different.

Why This Approach Works

Educational selling works for several reasons:

First, building trust is harder than building demand — but more valuable. You taught the other person something, gave them something useful — even if they don't buy this time, they remember you, and they'll refer others. That chain of trust is something advertising can't buy.

Second, what you learn from each conversation makes your product better. The friction customers name when they answer "why haven't you solved this problem yet?" is the best basis for prioritizing your features. No large-scale surveys needed — a few dozen one-on-one conversations and patterns emerge.

Third, a low-barrier start makes it easier to enter a real closing conversation. Educational conversations feel less pressured than traditional sales, so the other person is more willing to speak honestly — and their honest words are exactly what you need.

The Road to the First Hundred Customers

Lavingia says the Minimalist Entrepreneur's goal is to serve the first hundred customers well — not all at once, but one by one, personally serving, personally talking, personally teaching.

This process is slow, but what you build isn't just a customer base — it's a repeatable script: you know what questions customers will ask, which explanations are most effective, which objections come up most often, and how to respond in a way that puts people at ease enough to pay.

That script is the foundation for everything that comes later — automated sales, content marketing. Without it, you don't know what to write, what to say, what to emphasize. With it, every article you write and every sales page you build is answering a real question.

Common Mistakes

"I should finish building the product before talking to customers."
Sales conversations don't need to wait for the product to be done. You can talk to potential customers about their problems right now — even offer services or consulting, build the relationship, and address the product later.

"If I teach too much, customers won't need to buy."
This is the most common misconception. People who truly understand the problem are more aware of how hard it is to solve — they're actually more willing to find a professional to help them. Educating customers isn't teaching them to solve it themselves; it's helping them understand the depth of the problem.

"I'm not good at sales, so I'm not cut out for entrepreneurship."
If you're good at teaching, explaining, and helping people understand something — you already have the most essential sales skill. Minimalist Entrepreneur sales was never about salesmanship; it's about communication and teaching ability.
