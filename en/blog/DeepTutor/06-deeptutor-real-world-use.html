DeepTutor in the Real World: Three Use Cases for Students, Engineers, and Researchers

Apr 20, 2026·8 min read

The first five articles built the DeepTutor map—you know what it is, how to install it, and what each feature does. This article is the navigation.

Three users from different backgrounds: a student preparing for exams, an engineer transitioning roles or leveling up, a researcher managing literature. Each has different core pain points, different usage priorities, and different typical workflows.

Find the one that most resembles you, and apply it directly.

Scenario 1: Student — Exam Preparation and Course Understanding

Core pain points: Understood it in class, but can't solve it on the exam; followed the textbook, but a different problem format and you're stuck; not enough review time, unsure what to prioritize.

Recommended Setup

TutorBot: Create a strict but patient "Socratic Tutor"—one that doesn't give you the answer directly, but guides you to think it through with questions. Many students find this style frustrating at first, but it's the method that genuinely reinforces understanding.

TutorBot personality setting:
"When I ask you a question, first ask me back: 'What's your current understanding?'
After I explain, help me find the blind spots in my thinking—don't explain it for me.
If I really can't articulate it, give me a hint. Don't give me the answer.
At the end of each conversation, ask what I learned today and have me summarize it in one sentence."

Knowledge Base: Upload course slides (PDF), key textbook summaries (Markdown), and past exam papers. Once indexed, Quiz mode can generate questions directly targeted to your specific course material.

Proactive reminders: Set a daily 30-minute review reminder before bed, including one question from that day's material.

Typical Workflow: Pre-Exam Sprint

Week 1: Build Understanding

After each class, use Chat mode to process what you learned:

"I learned about B-Tree Indexes today. My understanding is [your understanding].
What am I getting wrong, or what important details am I missing?"

Week 2: Practice Problems

Use Quiz mode with the knowledge base for 20–30 minutes of testing each day:

"(Using knowledge base: Database Course)
Give me five scenario-based questions on Transaction Isolation Levels,
slightly harder than the midterm. After I answer, identify which concept is my weakest."

Final Three Days: Mock Exam

Use Deep Solve mode to work through past exam questions one by one:

"This is question 3 from last year's final: [question]
My attempted solution is [your solution], but my answer is wrong.
Help me identify exactly where my reasoning broke down,
then give me a correct problem-solving framework."

Expected Outcomes

The core of this workflow is replacing "passive reading" with "active recall + immediate error correction." Combined with TutorBot's spaced repetition reminders, the memory density going into the exam will be noticeably different.

Scenario 2: Engineer — Career Transition or Skill Upgrade

Core pain points: Too much technical documentation to read, learning and forgetting, unsure what level the real interviews test, no time for systematic study with only fragmented windows available.

Recommended Setup

TutorBot: Create an "Interview Coach" style—direct, precision-demanding, simulates the way interviewers probe.

TutorBot personality setting:
"You are a senior engineer running a mock technical interview.
When I say 'quiz me,' ask a technical question relevant to my target role,
then follow up like an interviewer—if my answer is too shallow, keep probing for details.
When you evaluate my answer, directly state what wasn't good enough. No sugarcoating."

Knowledge Base:

Organize by technical domain:

Base 1: System Design (Designing Data-Intensive Applications notes, LeetCode system design breakdowns)
Base 2: Target Language Deep Dive (Go language spec, concurrency pattern summaries)
Base 3: Company Tech Blogs (target company engineering blog posts organized as Markdown)

Proactive reminders: Set a weekly "mock interview" reminder; 15-minute commute Quiz sessions daily.

Typical Workflow: System Design Interview Prep

Monday to Wednesday: Deep Dive by Topic

Use Deep Solve mode for in-depth study on one system design topic:

"(Using knowledge base: System Design)
I want to design a URL Shortener supporting 100 million users.
Help me analyze requirements and estimate scale, then design the system architecture step by step.
Every design decision should explain why, and what the trade-offs are."

Thursday: Test What You Learned This Week

Quiz mode, targeting the knowledge base:

"Based on the system design notes I've updated in my knowledge base this week,
give me two scenario questions on Consistency vs. Availability trade-offs.
Require me to answer using design case examples—pure theoretical explanations not accepted."

Friday or Weekend: Mock Interview

Tell TutorBot to enter interview mode:

"Start a 45-minute system design mock interview.
Choose your own question in the FAANG interview style.
Follow up with probing questions after I answer. At the end, score me and give improvement feedback."

Using Fragmented Time

Not every learning session needs to be long—TutorBot on Telegram works with commute windows:

  • Morning commute: TutorBot sends a quiz question on a concept from yesterday
  • You answer in a few sentences on your phone
  • TutorBot gives instant feedback

This rhythm requires no computer, no learning environment setup—active recall in fragmented time is far more effective than reading articles.

Scenario 3: Researcher — Literature Management and Knowledge Organization

Core pain points: Too many papers to retain, can't find sources when citing, want to write research notes but organizing is time-consuming, cross-paper concept connections are hard to make.

Recommended Setup

TutorBot: Create a "Research Partner" style—one that critically examines your understanding from multiple angles and habitually raises counterexamples and alternative explanations.

TutorBot personality setting:
"You are a researcher with strong critical thinking.
When I share a research finding or hypothesis, first ask: 'What counterexamples have you considered?'
Encourage me to think through multiple explanations rather than accepting the first plausible one.
Help me maintain rigorous academic thinking habits."

Knowledge Base: This is where researchers get the most value—upload paper PDFs and build a topic-organized research literature base.

Base structure:
- Core theory papers (foundational works)
- Latest developments from the past two years (by year)
- Cross-disciplinary intersection literature
- Your own research notes and hypotheses

Typical Workflow: Literature Review

Step 1: Ingest a new paper

Download the PDF, upload it to the appropriate knowledge base. After indexing, use Chat mode for a quick summary:

"(Using knowledge base: Primary Research Area)
Summarize this newly uploaded paper in 5 key points,
and identify which papers already in my knowledge base it directly relates to—
especially similarities and differences on [specific concept]."

Step 2: Deep comparative analysis

Use Research mode for cross-paper comparison:

"(Using knowledge base: Primary Research Area)
Analyze all papers in my knowledge base related to [research topic].
Organize the three main schools of thought and their differences, with representative citations for each.
Output format: comparison table + 2–3 sentence summary per perspective."

Step 3: Generate research notes

Use the Co-Writer editor to refine DeepTutor's comparative analysis into your own research notes—these notes can also be uploaded back to the knowledge base so future queries can draw on your synthesized work (not just the raw papers).

Step 4: Test your grasp of the literature

Before writing a paper, use Quiz to confirm you actually remember the key points:

"Based on the literature in my knowledge base, ask me three questions:
1. In [a specific scenario], which paper's method is most relevant? Why?
2. What is the core claim in [researcher]'s [specific paper]?
3. What is the key experimental difference between [two methods]?"

Writing Assistance

When drafting a literature review section:

"(Using knowledge base: Primary Research Area)
I need to write a literature review paragraph on [research topic].
Please draft a version with citations—only cite papers from my knowledge base,
not anything from your training data."

This ensures every citation is from a real paper you've verified and stored in your knowledge base—not something the AI hallucinated.

The Common Principle Across All Three Scenarios

Whether you're a student, engineer, or researcher, the effectiveness of DeepTutor ultimately comes down to one thing: whether you take persistent memory seriously.

The tool gives you the container for memory, but you have to actively fill it:

  • After learning a concept, tell TutorBot your understanding and let it remember
  • When you discover a blind spot, update TutorBot
  • Regularly maintain your knowledge base to keep RAG retrieval quality high

The more DeepTutor understands you, the more useful it becomes. That understanding is built together—it doesn't generate itself.
